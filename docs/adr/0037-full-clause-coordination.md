# 0037 — Full-clause coordination: a comma before "but"/"and"/"or"

Date: 2026-09-03
Status: proposed (tentative). Revises ADR 0021 and ADR 0004.

## Context

A writer needed one sentence for 2 different subjects. The old Grammar
rejected the comma before "but". The old Grammar accepted "the test fails
but the agent retries the request" and rejected "the test fails, but the
agent retries the request".

The rule of the writer Steven Pinker is precise. A comma opens a new
clause. A Coordination of 2 predicates keeps one subject. A Coordination
of 2 predicates does not open a new clause. A Coordination of 2 predicates
does not need a comma. "the rule adds words but removes an ambiguity" is
one example.

## Decision

The language has 3 conjunctions:
- "and"
- "or"
- "but"

The Coordination has 2 shapes. The first shape keeps one subject, so a
comma does not follow the first shape. A conjunction joins 2 predicates.
The second shape opens a new subject and needs a comma. A conjunction
joins 2 clauses.

The second shape is a fiat Sentence Shape. The second shape is not a part
of the first shape. A causal sentence uses the comma of a clause for the
word "so". A causal sentence uses the comma of a clause for the word
"because". No ambiguity follows the new rule.

If a new subject follows a conjunction, then the advice appears. A
Conditional keeps the old Ban of a Coordination inside a clause. A future
decision can revisit the Ban.

## Consequences

- A writer can join 2 different subjects in one sentence, and the comma
  marks the new clause.
- The old shape of a full clause becomes a Ban. A writer adds the comma or
  splits the sentence.
- The rewrite of the decisions used the second shape.
